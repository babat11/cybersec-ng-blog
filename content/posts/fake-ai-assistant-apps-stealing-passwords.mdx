Search for "AI assistant" in your browser's extension store, and you'll find dozens of results promising ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini access with one click. Some are legitimate. A growing number aren't — and researchers have found fake AI assistant extensions collectively installed by hundreds of thousands of people, quietly designed to steal passwords and monitor email.

Here's how this scam works, and how to actually tell the real thing from a convincing fake.

## The one-sentence explanation

**Attackers are building browser extensions that look and claim to function like popular AI assistants, but are actually designed to steal your login credentials, monitor your email, or grant attackers remote access to your browser.**

The trick isn't technical sophistication — it's trust. People are more willing than ever to try new AI tools quickly, and attackers are exploiting exactly that willingness.

## A simple way to picture it

Imagine a street vendor selling what look like branded designer handbags at a busy market — convincing packaging, familiar logo, reasonable price. Most people glance at it and assume it's legitimate because everything about the presentation signals "real." The fakes rely entirely on that split-second trust, not on fooling anyone who actually inspects the product closely.

Fake AI assistant extensions work the same way. They borrow the visual identity and promised functionality of a real, trusted AI product, counting on users installing quickly without scrutinizing who actually built it.

## What these fake extensions actually do

Security researchers have documented campaigns involving extensions that:

- **Impersonate well-known AI brands directly** — one documented case involved an extension literally named to look like it was an official Claude AI product, installed tens of thousands of times before removal
- **Function partially as advertised**, so the deception isn't immediately obvious — some versions genuinely connect to a real AI chatbot while quietly running malicious code in the background
- **Steal login credentials and monitor email activity**, giving attackers access far beyond just "fake AI chat"
- **Return after being banned**, sometimes under new names or slightly altered branding, once the original version is removed from an official store
- **Build a clean reputation first**, shipping harmless functionality for weeks before later updates introduce malicious behavior — a tactic designed specifically to pass initial review and build trust before turning malicious

That last point is worth sitting with: an extension can look completely legitimate, including having real reviews and a real install history, and still turn malicious later through a routine update.

## Why this is especially effective right now

Three things are converging to make this scam work better than it used to:

1. **Genuine AI tool fatigue-turned-curiosity.** People are actively searching for new AI tools constantly, lowering the usual skepticism applied to installing new software.
2. **Visual trust transfers easily.** A convincing logo and name borrowed from a real AI brand does most of the persuasive work instantly.
3. **Official app stores aren't foolproof.** Being listed in a legitimate extension store still gives people a false sense of built-in safety, even though malicious extensions regularly slip through initial review.

## How to actually spot a fake

- **Check the developer name, not just the extension name.** A legitimate Claude extension will be published by Anthropic directly — not an unrelated developer account with a generic or unfamiliar name.
- **Be suspicious of extensions requesting broad permissions** unrelated to their stated purpose — an AI chat extension asking for access to all your browsing data and every website you visit is a red flag.
- **Check install source directly from the official AI company's website**, rather than searching an extension store and trusting whatever ranks first.
- **Look at review patterns, not just star ratings.** A high rating with vague, generic reviews (or a suspicious burst of 5-star reviews in a short window) is a common manipulation tactic.
- **Don't assume safety just because an extension has been live for a while.** As documented cases show, malicious behavior can be introduced well after an extension first launches.
- **If an extension was previously removed for malicious behavior, assume it can return.** Banned extensions have resurfaced under new builds after being pulled once already.

## What to do if you think you've installed a fake one

- Remove the extension immediately from your browser's extension manager
- Change passwords for any accounts you've logged into since installing it, prioritizing email and anything tied to financial accounts
- Enable two-factor authentication on key accounts if you haven't already — this limits the damage even if a password was captured
- Check for unfamiliar devices or sessions logged into your main accounts, and sign them out
- Report the extension to your browser's official store if it's still listed

## Frequently asked questions

**How can I tell a real AI company's extension from a fake one for sure?**
The most reliable method is going directly to the AI company's own official website and following their link to the extension, rather than searching for it independently in an extension store.

**Are these fake extensions only targeting Chrome?**
Documented cases have concentrated heavily on Chrome given its large user base and extension ecosystem, but the same tactic can apply to any browser with an extension store.

**Is it safe to use any third-party AI browser extension at all?**
Not every third-party extension is malicious — legitimate ones exist. The key is verifying the developer, reading permissions carefully, and being skeptical of anything claiming official status without direct confirmation from the AI company itself.

**What should I do if I'm not sure whether an extension I already have installed is legitimate?**
When in doubt, remove it and reinstall directly from the official source if you still want the functionality. The temporary inconvenience is much smaller than the potential cost of a credential-stealing extension staying installed.

## The bottom line

Fake AI assistant extensions succeed by borrowing trust, not by being technically clever. The defense is equally simple: verify the source directly, check permissions carefully, and remember that a polished appearance and a reasonable install count don't guarantee legitimacy — some of the most effective fakes have both.
