Here's a number that says more about the current state of cybercrime than almost any headline could: **$50**. That's roughly what a researcher estimated it cost an attacker, per successful hack, in a recent AI-powered hacking campaign.

Not $50,000. Not even $500. Fifty dollars — about the price of a decent pair of shoes — to successfully break into a target. If that number doesn't sit right with you, it shouldn't. It's the real story behind why AI is reshaping cybercrime, and it has almost nothing to do with robots or science fiction.

## The one-sentence explanation

**AI tools have dramatically cut the time, skill, and money required to carry out a successful cyberattack — turning what used to be a specialized, expensive operation into something closer to a cheap, repeatable business.**

This is the part that gets lost in flashier AI-threat headlines. It's not that AI invented new kinds of attacks out of thin air. It's that AI made the old ones dramatically cheaper to run at scale.

## Why cost actually matters in cybersecurity

It's tempting to think of hacking as a binary — either someone wants to attack you specifically, or they don't. In reality, a huge share of cyberattacks are driven by simple economics. If an attack costs more time and effort than the payoff is worth, most attackers move on. If it becomes cheap enough to automate and repeat endlessly, the math changes completely.

That's exactly what's happening. When the cost per attack drops to something like $50, an attacker doesn't need to carefully pick one high-value target. They can afford to attack hundreds or thousands of targets and simply accept that most will fail, because the ones that succeed more than cover the cost.

## What's actually driving the cost down

**1. Automated reconnaissance**
Researching a target — finding exposed systems, weak points, employee information — used to take real human time. AI tools can now do much of this research automatically, at a speed and scale no human team could match manually.

**2. AI-assisted exploit writing**
Writing working malicious code used to require real technical skill. AI coding tools can now help generate, adapt, and troubleshoot attack code, lowering the skill floor required to carry out technically sophisticated attacks.

**3. Automated, continuous attacks**
Security researchers have documented fully automated hacking services that use AI tools to continuously attack large numbers of websites at a low per-attack cost — essentially industrializing what used to be manual, one-at-a-time hacking work.

**4. Scalable social engineering**
As covered in our [AI threats post](/blog?category=ai-threats), AI can generate convincing phishing messages and scam content at a volume no human scammer could write alone, multiplying the number of attempts for roughly the same effort.

## Why this changes who you should actually worry about

The traditional mental image of "a hacker" — a highly skilled individual targeting you specifically — was never the most common real-world threat, but it's become even less relevant now. The more realistic threat in 2027 is automated, low-cost, high-volume: an AI-assisted system probing thousands of targets, not caring which ones specifically respond, just collecting whichever ones do.

This matters because it changes the defense mindset. You're less likely to be the target of a determined individual and more likely to be one of thousands of targets an automated system is testing cheaply and continuously, simply because you happened to be reachable.

## How to protect yourself against cheap, high-volume attacks

- **Patch and update consistently.** Automated attacks overwhelmingly target known, unpatched vulnerabilities — they're not usually worth the effort of finding something novel when easy targets exist.
- **Use unique passwords and enable two-factor authentication everywhere you can.** Low-cost automated attacks rely heavily on credential stuffing and reused passwords; this single habit defeats a huge share of them.
- **Don't assume you're "too small to be a target."** Automated, cheap attacks don't discriminate by size or importance — if you're reachable and vulnerable, you're a viable target regardless of how interesting you are.
- **Be skeptical of unusually convincing phishing attempts.** As the cost of generating convincing scam content drops, the old advice of "watch for bad grammar" matters less than ever.
- **For businesses: assume continuous probing, not occasional attacks.** Security postures built around "preventing a determined attacker" should increasingly also account for constant, low-effort automated attempts.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does this mean AI has made hacking accessible to anyone?**
It's lowered the skill floor significantly for certain types of attacks, particularly ones that rely on automation and scale rather than deep technical novelty. It hasn't eliminated the need for skill entirely, but it has meaningfully widened who can participate.

**Is this $50 figure typical, or an extreme example?**
It's one documented estimate from a specific researcher's analysis of one campaign, not a universal price tag. The broader point it illustrates — that AI has meaningfully reduced attack costs — is reflected across multiple separate reports and incidents, even if the exact number varies by situation.

**Does this mean traditional security advice is outdated?**
Not at all — the fundamentals (patching, unique passwords, two-factor authentication, skepticism toward urgent requests) matter more, not less, precisely because the volume of automated attempts testing for those exact weaknesses has gone up.

**Should small businesses and individuals be more worried than before?**
The relevant shift is less about being more worried and more about recognizing you're competing against automated volume rather than a single human adversary's limited time and attention — which actually makes consistent basic hygiene more valuable, not less.

## The bottom line

The scariest thing about AI and cybercrime isn't a dramatic new kind of attack — it's the plain economics of a $50 hack. When the cost of attempting an attack drops that low, volume replaces precision, and basic security hygiene becomes less optional than ever, because you're no longer defending against effort, you're defending against scale.
