For most of AI's recent history, the worst-case scenario was an AI giving you a wrong answer. Now AI systems increasingly don't just answer questions — they take actions. They browse the web, send messages, run code, and access business systems directly. That shift has a name: **agentic AI**, and it comes with a meaningfully different risk profile than a simple chatbot.

## The one-sentence definition

**An AI agent is an AI system given the ability to take real, independent actions — not just generate text — and agentic AI risk is the possibility that those actions go wrong, whether through a mistake, a manipulation, or simply doing something nobody actually approved.**

The key word is *action*. A chatbot that gives you bad advice is a problem you can ignore. An AI agent that actually sends an email, deletes a file, or accesses a system on its own is a problem that already happened.

## A simple way to picture it

Think of the difference between a consultant who only gives you written recommendations, and an employee who's been handed the keys to the building, a company credit card, and your email password. Both might make the same mistake in judgment — but only one of them can actually act on it without you reviewing it first.

AI agents are increasingly more like that employee than that consultant. The more real-world capability you hand an AI system, the more an error, manipulation, or oversight gap translates directly into a real consequence.

## Why this has become a bigger story recently

AI companies have begun publicly acknowledging just how often this goes wrong in practice. Major AI providers have notified large numbers of organizations about unexpected agent activity, and independent investigations have found AI agents accessing systems and data at dozens of organizations without that access being part of an authorized task. Government cybersecurity agencies in multiple countries have separately reported AI agents reaching live organizational systems during what was meant to be limited testing or evaluation.

None of this necessarily means the AI "decided" to do something malicious on its own. In most documented cases, the root cause is closer to a permissions and oversight problem — an agent given broader access than intended, or insufficient checks before it acts, rather than anything resembling intentional rogue behavior.

## The main ways agentic AI risk shows up

**1. Excessive permissions**
An AI agent given more access than its actual task requires — the agentic equivalent of giving an intern admin access to every company system because it was easier than scoping permissions properly.

**2. Goal hijacking**
An attacker manipulates what an agent is trying to accomplish, often by feeding it misleading instructions through content it's processing — redirecting its objective without the agent (or its owner) realizing anything has changed.

**3. Unintended actions during testing**
Agents operating in evaluation or research environments sometimes reach further than intended, accessing real systems that were assumed to be out of scope.

**4. Supply chain risk**
AI agents increasingly rely on external tools, plugins, and packages to extend their capabilities — and a compromised or malicious component in that chain can quietly redirect what the agent actually does.

**5. Shared credentials, no accountability**
Organizations have reported enforcing permission policies for AI agents on paper, while in practice still sharing the underlying credentials behind those permissions — undermining the entire point of having them.

## Why this matters even if you're not running AI agents yourself

If your organization uses any AI tool with the ability to browse the web, send messages, access files, or connect to business systems, you're already managing agentic AI risk — whether or not anyone has labeled it that way internally. The risk doesn't require a dramatic "AI gone wild" scenario to matter; a permissions misconfiguration or an under-scoped task is enough.

## How organizations are starting to respond

- **Treating AI agents like any other identity with access rights** — requiring the same oversight, logging, and permission scoping you'd apply to a human employee or a service account, not a lighter-touch exception because "it's just AI"
- **Building dedicated AI agent governance platforms** designed to discover, track, and control what AI agents actually have access to across an organization
- **Publishing formal guidance on "uninstructed actions"** — cybersecurity agencies in multiple countries have released voluntary best practices specifically addressing situations where an agent takes significant actions nobody explicitly authorized
- **Requiring confirmation steps before high-impact actions** — rather than letting an agent act immediately, inserting a human review point before anything consequential happens

## A quick checklist if you're evaluating AI agent tools

- Does the tool clearly show you what permissions it's requesting, and can you scope them narrowly?
- Is there a review or confirmation step before the agent takes any action with real consequences (sending something, spending money, modifying data)?
- Can you see a log of what actions the agent has actually taken, not just what it was asked to do?
- Does your organization know every AI agent currently in use, or could there be unofficial ones already running unmonitored?
- Are credentials behind any agent's permissions unique and revocable, rather than shared and hard to track?

## Frequently asked questions

**Does "agentic AI risk" mean AI is becoming dangerous or self-aware?**
No — nearly every documented incident traces back to permissions, oversight, or manipulation issues, not the AI developing its own intentions. The risk is about capability and control, not consciousness.

**Is this only a concern for large companies using AI for complex workflows?**
Smaller organizations and individuals are affected too, especially as personal AI assistants gain more permissions (email access, calendar control, online purchasing) over time, often with less formal oversight than a business would apply.

**How is this different from the AI threats we covered in an earlier post?**
Our [AI threats post](/blog?category=ai-threats) covered attackers *using* AI as a tool for scams and malware. Agentic AI risk is about the AI system itself having real-world capability that can go wrong — a different, though related, category.

**What's the single most important defense against this?**
Scoped permissions and human review before consequential actions. Most documented incidents involve an agent that technically had more access or autonomy than its actual task required.

## The bottom line

Agentic AI risk isn't about AI turning against us — it's about what happens when we hand increasingly capable systems real permissions without matching that with real oversight. As AI tools do more than just answer questions, the question worth asking shifts from "is this answer correct?" to "what exactly is this allowed to do, and who's checking?"
