When people hear "AI threats," they often picture something out of a movie — rogue robots, superintelligent systems turning against us. The real threats in 2027 are far less dramatic, and far more likely to actually land in your inbox.

This post covers the AI threats that are realistically affecting regular people and businesses right now — no sci-fi required.

## The one-sentence definition

**An AI threat is any attack that uses artificial intelligence to make scams, fraud, or malicious software more convincing, more targeted, or harder to detect.**

Notice what's missing from that definition: AI deciding to attack on its own. Almost every AI threat you'll actually encounter in 2027 still has a human attacker behind it — AI is just making their job easier.

## 1. Deepfakes — AI-generated fake audio and video

A deepfake is AI-generated media designed to look or sound like a real person saying or doing something they never actually said or did.

**Why it matters in 2027:** Deepfake audio is now convincing enough to be used in phone scams — a cloned voice of a company executive calling an employee to urgently request a wire transfer, or a cloned voice of a family member claiming to be in trouble. The technology to do this convincingly no longer requires special expertise.

**What to watch for:** Urgency plus an unusual request is the biggest tell, regardless of how real the voice or video sounds. If a call or video demands immediate money transfer, password sharing, or secrecy, treat it with the same suspicion you'd apply to any unexpected request — verify through a separate channel before acting.

## 2. AI-generated phishing and scam messages

Phishing used to be easy to spot — bad grammar, awkward phrasing, obviously fake sender names. AI has mostly erased those tells.

**Why it matters in 2027:** Attackers can now generate phishing emails that are grammatically perfect, personalized using publicly available information, and written in a tone that matches a real colleague or company. Volume has also gone up — AI lets attackers generate thousands of unique, convincing messages instead of copy-pasting one template.

**What to watch for:** Since "bad writing" is no longer a reliable red flag, focus instead on the request itself: unexpected password resets, unusual payment requests, or links asking you to log in somewhere unfamiliar. Hover before you click, and verify unusual requests independently.

## 3. AI-generated malware

This doesn't mean "AI that attacks on its own" — it means attackers using AI tools to help write malicious code faster, adapt it to evade detection, or generate variations of existing malware at a pace that would have taken a human team much longer.

**Why it matters in 2027:** Security teams are seeing malware that changes its own code slightly with each version to slip past detection tools trained on older patterns — and AI tools make generating those variations fast and cheap.

**What to watch for:** This one's mostly invisible to regular users, which is exactly why the basics still matter most: keep software updated, don't download attachments or run files from untrusted sources, and rely on security tools that focus on behavior (what a program actually does) rather than just matching known malware signatures.

## 4. Fake AI tools and lookalike apps

As real AI tools have become popular, so have fake ones — malicious apps or browser extensions pretending to be a popular AI assistant, designed to steal data or credentials instead.

**Why it matters in 2027:** The rush to try new AI tools means people are more willing than usual to download unfamiliar apps or paste sensitive information into unfamiliar websites.

**What to watch for:** Only download AI tools from official sources, double-check URLs before pasting anything sensitive, and be skeptical of AI tools that seem to appear overnight with suspiciously aggressive marketing.

## What all four of these have in common

None of these threats require the AI itself to be malicious. In every case, AI is a force multiplier for a human attacker — making their scam more convincing, their malware more adaptive, or their reach wider. Understanding that distinction matters, because it tells you where your actual defenses should go: not "can I trust this AI," but "can I verify this request through another channel."

## A quick checklist

- Treat any urgent request for money, passwords, or secrecy as suspicious — regardless of how real the voice, video, or message looks
- Verify unusual requests through a second channel (call back on a known number, message a colleague directly)
- Judge phishing attempts by the request, not the writing quality
- Only download AI tools from official, verified sources
- Keep software and security tools updated — adaptive malware is exactly what signature-based detection struggles with

## Frequently asked questions

**Are deepfakes illegal?**
Laws vary by country and are still catching up to the technology. Using a deepfake for fraud or impersonation is illegal in most jurisdictions even where deepfakes themselves aren't explicitly regulated.

**How can I tell if a voice on the phone is a deepfake?**
It's getting genuinely difficult to tell by ear alone. The more reliable defense is verification habits — calling back on a known number — rather than trying to detect the fake in the moment.

**Is AI making cybercrime worse overall?**
It's making existing attacks faster, more convincing, and more scalable rather than inventing entirely new categories of crime. The fundamentals of scams and malware haven't changed — the execution has gotten much better.

**Do I need special software to protect against AI threats?**
Good security hygiene — updated software, skepticism toward urgent requests, verifying through separate channels — still covers most of this. Specialized AI-aware security tools matter more for businesses managing AI systems directly.

## The bottom line

The AI threats worth actually worrying about in 2027 aren't exotic — they're familiar scams and malware, just faster, more convincing, and harder to spot by instinct alone. The defense isn't fundamentally different either: verify before you trust, especially when something feels urgent.
